Where Are the Big Dogs?

Research behind the story of giant prehistoric canids, why the bone-crushing dogs disappeared, and why extinct cats became cultural icons while most extinct dogs did not.

32M+years of borophagine history in North America
60+species in the bone-crushing dog radiation
7 vs. 0wild-cat vs. wild-canid mascots across current SWAC + MEAC schools

The big dogs were real.

Epicyon haydeni

The Florida Museum describes Epicyon as the largest known canid. It belonged to the extinct Borophaginae—the “bone-crushing dogs.”

Paleoart: A. James Gustafson.

Borophagus

A late bone-crushing canid with powerful jaws. Coprolites containing bone fragments provide direct evidence of heavy bone consumption.

Image: U.S. National Park Service / Reid Psaltis.

Works cited

  • Florida Museum of Natural History. “Epicyon haydeni.” Background on borophagine dogs and Epicyon as the largest known canid.
  • Silvestro, Daniele, et al. “The Role of Clade Competition in the Diversification of North American Canids.” PNAS, 2015. Fossil analysis examining competition and extinction across North American canid lineages.
  • Wang, Xiaoming, et al. “First Bone-Cracking Dog Coprolites Provide New Insight into Bone Consumption in Borophagus…” eLife, 2018. Direct fossil evidence for bone consumption by Borophagus.
  • Gonyea, W. J., and R. Ashworth. “The Form and Function of Retractile Claws in the Felidae and Other Representative Carnivorans.” Research on the anatomy and predatory function of retractile claws in felids.
  • Colléony, Agathe, et al. “Human Preferences for Species Conservation: Animal Charisma Trumps Endangered Status.” Biological Conservation, 2017. Evidence that charisma and familiarity affect the public attention animals receive.
  • Southwestern Athletic Conference. Member Institutions / SWAC History. Current SWAC membership used for the mascot comparison.
  • Mid-Eastern Athletic Conference. Current Member Institutions. Current MEAC membership used for the mascot comparison.
  • National Park Service. “The Bone-Crusher (Borophagus hilli).” Accessible background and paleoart for a well-known bone-crushing dog.
